Inner Beast

Genre 3D Action Adventure
Type Blockout
Engine Unreal Engine 5
Tools Unreal GASP · Max Pears' Level Design Kit
Role Level Designer
Stats 8 iterations · 6 playtests
Play Now

Level Overview

Inner Beast is a linear stealth level set in a post-apocalyptic natural reserve, a world where nature has reclaimed every street and building since a catastrophic event unleashed paranormal phenomena across the land.

The level is designed as an experience that escalates in tension across nine beats, from a slow introduction where the player learns movement and social stealth, through a parkour crossing over a collapsed bridge, into the reserve where a giant wolf spirit is first encountered, and finally to a climactic confrontation inside a nature-consumed research station.

The core design challenge was building a believable escalation of dread using only a blockout, no art assets, no lighting polish, relying entirely on spatial composition, enemy patrol logic, environmental puzzle placement and pacing to communicate tone and tension.

Level Pillars

Interweaved traversal sections optimized for GASP controller
GASP was chosen deliberately over more feature-rich controllers precisely because of its constraints. Where other controllers offer climbing, ropes, swimming and combat, GASP keeps it to jumping, vaulting, crouching and ledge grabbing, and that limitation became a design tool. Every traversal sequence was built around making those four actions feel purposeful, forcing the design to be interesting through spatial composition rather than mechanical complexity.
Smooth transitions between urban and natural areas
The level moves the player through three distinct environments: a post-apocalyptic residential shelter, a collapsed bridge, and an overgrown natural reserve, without hard breaks between them. Each transition was designed to feel earned spatially: the urban geometry gradually gives way to eroded surfaces and encroaching vegetation, reinforcing the narrative shift from the familiar into the unknown.
High tension, non-violent stealth encounters
There are no combat mechanics in Inner Beast. When an enemy detects the player, a simple trigger teleports them to the nearest checkpoint and resets enemy positions. This deliberate simplicity kept the focus entirely on the level design: tension is built through patrol placement, sightlines, cover availability and pacing, not through systems complexity. Every tense moment is earned spatially.
Rewarding physics-based puzzles
The puzzles follow the same philosophy as the rest of the level: no plugins, no complex scripting. Pushing a crate to reach a ledge, repositioning an object to create cover from a patrol. The reward comes not from solving an elaborate system but from reading the space correctly and using what's already there.

Level Areas

All areas and their key moments mapped
The Shelter
The level's opening area. An abandoned building repurposed as a community refuge. The player learns movement and social stealth by navigating around guards. Getting caught here simply turns you around, light enough to encourage experimentation before the real tension begins.
Collapsed Bridge
The transition between the urban world and the reserve. A parkour-focused section that demands the player apply everything learned in the shelter, while offering the first unobstructed view of the reserve ahead: a deliberate contemplative beat before tension escalates.
Wolves Den
The reserve's interior, where the giant wolf spirit is first encountered. The player must navigate sightlines, use cover and interact with the environment to avoid detection. The point where stealth stops being safe and starts being genuinely threatening.
Research Station
The level's final area. The exterior of a nature-consumed research building, featuring physics-based puzzles that gate access to the entrance. The level culminates here with the climactic encounter with the wolf spirit.

Gameplay Highlights

Finding Clues
The player explores the level in search of information about the missing brother, interacting with the environment and NPCs to piece together what happened.
Sneaking Around
Core stealth gameplay in action: navigating patrol routes, using cover and crouching to stay out of enemies' sightlines without any combat mechanics to fall back on.
Finding Treasures
Optional exploration that rewards the curious player: hidden narrative pickups and collectibles scattered across the reserve that deepen the world without blocking progress.
Solving Environmental Puzzles
Physics-based interactions using only what's already in the level: pushing crates, repositioning objects, designed to feel intuitive and rewarding without relying on complex scripting.

The Creative Process

Research and ideation

Game References

The research phase started with three game levels analyzed in detail: The Last of Us Part II (Birthday Gift), Final Fantasy XV (Friends of a Feather), and What Remains of Edith Finch (Introduction). Each was studied for lighting, layout, tone and adaptation potential, not to replicate them, but to extract specific techniques worth borrowing.

Three key lessons shaped Inner Beast's design direction: the power of dramatic lighting shifts to change emotional register within a single level; the value of framing and composition as navigation tools; and the impact of claustrophobic layouts when sneaking around a large, threatening enemy.

Real-world Photography

These photographs were taken a few steps from where I lived at the time: the daily walking route with my dog along a wetland path running beside a residential block. Watching nature and the urban environment bleed into each other every day was the direct spark for Inner Beast's setting, and a significant part of the blockout is a direct spatial adaptation of this place.

Visual References

A curated set of internet references gathered specifically for lighting, atmosphere and mood. The focus was on fog density, light filtering through trees, the contrast between natural and decayed man-made structures, and the visual language of ominous, overgrown environments.

2D Map and layout

The sketching process started from the real geography: the way the residential area where I lived connected to the wetland path behind it. Rather than designing from scratch, I mapped how those two zones actually related spatially and worked through several iterations trying to preserve that logic in a playable form: how the urban geometry gives way to the natural path, where the chokepoints fall, and how to make the route feel earned without being arbitrary. The final layout kept the essential spatial relationship of the real place.

First Blockout

The first blockout started with a flat plane in Unreal with the 2D map projected onto it, walked through it on foot to validate scale and feel before building anything. Once the proportions felt right, rough volumes were placed at each point of interest to confirm their sizes and positions relative to each other. Only after that spatial logic was locked in did the detailed per-area blocking begin.

Iterative process

Over six weeks of playtests and peer feedback within the bootcamp, the level went through eight iterations focused on a consistent set of problems: how freely the player moved through the space, where friction appeared unintentionally, and how clearly the intended path read without explicit direction. Each session surfaced new issues: a sightline that confused rather than guided, a cover placement that broke the patrol logic, a transition that felt abrupt. Each iteration was an attempt to resolve them through the blockout alone, without adding new mechanics or systems. The goal was always the same: make the level guide the player silently, through space and composition rather than UI or text.

Polish and final touches

The polish phase is arguably the most important, and the most surprising. After eight iterations of spatial problem-solving, it's the small details that carry the level across the finish line: a texture swap that suddenly makes a surface readable, a light aimed at the right angle that turns a dead corner into a landmark, an affordance that tells the player exactly what they can interact with without a single line of UI, or a small scripted moment that makes the world feel inhabited rather than constructed. None of these changes touch the layout, but all of them change how the level feels to play. A blockout that communicates its intent clearly through space and detail is a fundamentally different experience from one that doesn't. The gap between the two is almost entirely made up of these small, deliberate choices.

Final thoughts

Inner Beast was a deliberate exercise in constraints. Choosing a simple character controller, avoiding complex scripting, limiting myself to basic physics interactions: every one of those decisions forced the design to be more interesting, not less. When you can't rely on systems to carry the experience, the space has to do the work.

The iterative process was humbling. What felt finished after the first blockout looked rough after the first playtest, and what felt finished after the fourth iteration still had problems that only became visible when someone else played it. Six playtests across eight iterations taught me that level design is fundamentally a communication problem: you're trying to make a player feel and do things without ever talking to them directly, and that no amount of solo playthroughs substitutes for watching someone else navigate your space for the first time.

The biggest lesson came from the natural environment sections. Laying out organic, open spaces is a fundamentally different challenge from designing urban or architectural ones, and my 2D map for the reserve was essentially useless once I got into the engine. The paths, the sightlines, the sense of openness: none of it translated from paper to 3D the way a building floor plan would. I spent a significant amount of time stuck, improvising in the blockout without a solid foundation to build from. That delay cascaded into the final section of the level, which never received the attention it needed: the research station interior was never completed, and the narrative ending I had envisioned never made it into the build.

That's the thing I'd do differently: spend more time understanding how to plan natural environments before touching the engine. The constraint that cost me most wasn't technical. It was a gap in my process.
